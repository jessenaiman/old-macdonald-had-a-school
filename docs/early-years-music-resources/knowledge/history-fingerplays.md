# A History of Fingerplays — From Unconscious Folk Play to Library Lap

Synthesized 2026-08-07 from primary sources in the collection
(`04-historical-public-domain/`, `07-historical-more/`, folders 01–03, 05–09).
Path convention: relative to `docs/early-years-music-resources/`.
Book page numbers refer to the printed edition; for scanned PDFs without a
text layer, read_file renders pages as images.

## The arc in one paragraph

Fingerplays begin before print as unconscious folk hand-play (Pat-a-cake,
"Here is the church"). **Froebel (1844)** turns them into intentional
pedagogy — the mother's lap as first classroom, the hand as the child's first
instrument. The **kindergarten movement** carries them to America, where by
**1887** they have a labeled shelf (Walker & Jenks) and by **1893** a
dedicated book and a coined, trademarked name (Poulsson). Folklorists of the 1880s–90s
(Gomme, Newell, Green) simultaneously collect the older hand-games as
antiquities. The 1914 Quigley index shows the canon hardened across
publishers. In the 20th–21st centuries the formal kindergarten fades and the
fingerplays migrate into **preschool circle time and library storytime**,
where the same plays survive with a new stated rationale: theology →
physiology → neuroscience. The repertoire outlives every theory used to
explain it.

## Timeline

### Before print — hand-play older than any book
- Poulsson's 1893 preface: "In all times and among all nations, finger-plays
  have been a delight of childhood", citing as already-ancient Pat-a-cake and
  "Here is the church, / And here's the steeple"
  (`04-historical-public-domain/pdf/poulsson-finger-plays-nursery-kindergarten-1893.pdf`,
  Preface).
- The folklorist ("survivalist") school treats children's games as relics of
  ancient custom: Gomme's dictionary records hand-games **Handy Dandy**,
  **Chickidy Hand**, **Hand in and Hand out** and repeatedly argues antiquity
  and ritual survival (`04-historical-public-domain/doc/gutenberg-gomme-traditional-games-england-scotland-ireland-vol1-1894.txt`).
  Whether or not the survival theory holds, a clapping game was considered
  old enough to be a historical source.

### 1765 / 1843 — first printed strata of the rhymes themselves
- `04-historical-public-domain/pdf/newbery-original-mother-gooses-melody-1889.pdf`
  — facsimile of Newbery's *Mother Goose's Melody* (c. 1765), the first
  printed Mother Goose.
- `04-historical-public-domain/doc/gutenberg-only-true-mother-goose-melodies-1843.txt`
  (~lines 1006–1016) — American chapbook Pat-a-cake: "Pat it, and prick it, /
  And mark it with **T**. / And then it will serve / For **Tommy** and me."
  The letter-swap variation already exists in 1843.

### 1844 — Froebel makes lap-play intentional
- `04-historical-public-domain/pdf/froebel-mother-play-nursery-songs-1906.pdf`
  (American ed. 1878/1906, trans. Dwight & Jarvis, ed. **Elizabeth P.
  Peabody**, of *Mutter- und Kose-Lieder*, 1844).
- Finger-exercise curriculum in the TOC (book pp. 5–6): **Play with the
  Limbs; Pat-a-cake (No. 12, p. 49); This Little Thumb (No. 16, p. 61); The
  Finger Game (p. 65); Grandmother and Mother (p. 67); The Little Thumb is
  One (p. 69); The Piano-Forte (p. 70); Up and Down the Fingers Go (p. 71);
  Brothers and Sisters (p. 73)**; plus **Notes to Mothers (p. 159)** — the
  original "adult is the student" apparatus.
- Key ideas, verbatim: "These are the fingers and hands made for play: / My
  darling shall learn how to use them one day" (Mother's Self-Communings);
  "Counting is a noble art, / That man is wont to underrate" (The Little
  Thumb is One); "Early the child divines aright, / That several parts in one
  whole unite" (Grandmother and Mother — five fingers as the family).
- Epigraph that defines the era, quoted by Poulsson from Froebel: "**What the
  child imitates, he begins to understand.**"

### 1860s–1880s — the kindergarten movement carries it to America
- Peabody's American Preface: the book is "brought out in America in answer
  to a wide demand of American mothers"; rhythmical motion and music are
  "universal as humanity". Peabody is the editor of the American edition and
  a central figure in bringing the kindergarten to the US.
- `04-historical-public-domain/pdf/jenks-songs-games-little-ones-1887.pdf`
  (Walker & Jenks; contents page, PDF pp. ~7–10) — a section literally titled
  **FINGER PLAYS**: *Eight white
  Sheep; Five little Chickadees; Fly, little Birds; In the Branches of a Tree;
  Little Boy Blue; My Pigeon House; The Family; The Little Mice are creeping;
  The Pigeon Song; Thumbkin says, "I'll dance."* The genre has a shelf label
  six years before Poulsson's book.

### 1893 — Poulsson names the genre and writes its canon
- `04-historical-public-domain/pdf/poulsson-finger-plays-nursery-kindergarten-1893.pdf`
  — *Finger Plays for Nursery and Kindergarten*, Emilie Poulsson, music
  Cornelia C. Roeske, illus. L.J. Bridgman (Lothrop, Lee & Shepard, Boston).
- Title page: "**FINGER PLAYS. TRADE-MARK. Registered in U.S. Patent
  Office.**" — the term itself was a coined commercial category in 1893.
- First plain developmental rationale (Preface): beyond pleasure, fingerplays
  assist "the development of strength and flexibility in the tiny lax
  fingers", and "dormant thought may receive its first awakening call through
  the motions which interpret as well as illustrate" the words.
- First book to span the age range: a ten-month-old plays "All for Baby"
  "pounding and clapping gleefully"; seven- and eight-year-olds sing "The
  Caterpillar" and "How the Corn Grew". Preface describes the choreography of
  "The Merry Little Men" — the ten fingers appearing, named in turn,
  fluttering (the five-fingers-as-characters formula Froebel started).

### 1883–1899 — folklorists collect the older hand-games
- Newell *Games and Songs of American Children* (1883; 1903 ed.),
  `.../doc/newell-games-songs-american-children-1903.txt`; Gomme 1894/98;
  Green *A History of Nursery Rhymes* 1899
  (`04-historical-public-domain/pdf/green-history-of-nursery-rhymes-1899.pdf`).

### 1914 — the canon hardens
- `04-historical-public-domain/doc/gutenberg-quigley-index-kindergarten-songs-1914.txt`
  (lines ~8999–9005): **Pat-a-cake** indexed across *Froebel (MP), Gaynor
  (LL), Hubbard (MSG), Smith (SL1), Brewster (BSS)* — five independent
  songbooks carrying the same play. That is what a canon looks like in the
  archival record.

### 20th–21st c. — from kindergarten circle to library lap
The formal Froebelian kindergarten fades; the fingerplays migrate into
preschool circle time and library storytime. Same plays, new rationale:
- 1844 Froebel: cosmic unity; counting "a noble art".
- 1893 Poulsson: strength/flexibility of fingers; imitation awakens thought.
- 1990s–2020s: Feierabend files fingerplays under "Non-Locomotor … movement
  for form and expression" in a music-readiness curriculum
  (`09-educators-performers-more/pdf/feierabend-music-movement-early-years-handout.pdf`,
  pp. 1–2, 9 — incl. **Two Little Blackbirds**, thumbs as Jack and Jill);
  Head Start frames *Where Is Thumbkin?* as fine-motor training for the hand
  that will hold a pencil; libraries frame rhymes as phonological awareness.

## Why the history matters

1. Fingerplays are the oldest continuously used curriculum material in early
   education — the same hand-plays run 1765 → 1843 → 1893 → 2020s handouts.
2. They are inherently duet-form: the adult's hands guide the child's (the
   original "lapsit" program).
3. They are zero-cost, zero-prep, and fully inclusive — they live in people,
   not equipment, which is why they survived every institutional change.
4. Built-in variation (letters, sizes, names) *is* the pedagogy; expect and
   invite mutation.
