# Felt Chromatics — A Design Philosophy

**The movement:** Felt Chromatics. A colour register for a felt schoolhouse — where every character is a swatch, every swatch is a character, and the whole cast hangs together like a patchwork quilt stitched from one bolt of cloth.

## The philosophy

Felt Chromatics treats colour the way a naturalist treats specimens: collected, labelled, and pinned in careful rows so that the invisible structure of a community becomes visible. Sixteen characters become sixteen circular patches — each one a face, each one a hue — arranged not as decoration but as evidence. The viewer does not read about the school's identity; they see its entire cast assembled in a single disciplined field, the way a lepidopterist pins a family of butterflies. This is a scientific plate for an imaginary institution, and it must carry the reverence of one.

Space and form obey the patch. Each character lives inside a perfect circle — the felt badge — repeated with mechanical consistency so that difference reads as identity, not accident. The grid is strict: same radius, same rhythm, same quiet margin between neighbours. Within that discipline, the faces themselves carry all the warmth: a duck's grin, a cow's bell, a farmer's whiskers. Form is the cage that makes the personality sing. Nothing overlaps, nothing drifts, nothing is centred by eye alone — every circle sits where geometry demands, with the stillness of something pressed in a book for years.

Colour and material are one act. The background of each badge is dyed felt: a flat, matte colour field with just enough grain to feel touched by hand, never rendered with gradients or gloss. The palette is deliberately primary and early-years — barn red, sky blue, meadow green, sunny yellow — colours a child can name before they can read. Each hue is assigned like a name: the teachers own the saturated anchors, and the learners inherit those same hues shifted in value, lighter and softer, so the children read as the teachers' colours seen through morning light. Sixteen characters, eight hue families, one community.

Scale and rhythm make the register feel hand-made and exhaustive at once. The patches are large enough to meet the eye as portraits, small enough that the whole cast fits in one gaze. Reference numbers, hex codes, and role labels sit in a quiet mono type beside each swatch, like museum tags — present but never shouting. The composition breathes in even measures: title, cast, legend, margin. A single thin rule separates teachers from learners. The eye travels the grid the way a hand would run across a quilt: row by row, patch by patch, until the whole school is known.

Composition and balance hold the register together with the patience of a master craftsperson. The piece must read as the work of someone at the absolute top of their field: the stitching implied in each felt edge, the hairline rules measured to the pixel, the type set with painstaking attention, every hex code double-checked against the source of truth. The negative space around the grid is not emptiness — it is the felt board the patches are pinned to. The final work should feel like it took countless hours, because the illusion of effortless order is the product of deep expertise, and the viewer should sense, without being told, that a person laboured over this plate with the care of someone making a family heirloom.

Text is sparse and clinical, never decorative. A title names the register. A formula explains the learner shift in one line — `student = teacher · value shift` — the way a caption explains a specimen. Names label the faces. Hex codes anchor every hue to its source. Nothing more. The information lives in the colour field itself: the viewer reads the community from the swatches before they read a single word, and the words only confirm what the felt already said.

The result is a contradiction held in perfect tension: a document that behaves like a scientific bible — dense, systematic, repeatable — while the subject is a barnyard of smiling animals and the teachers who love them. That paradox is the point. The most ephemeral thing in the world — a child's first classroom — is here mapped, labelled, and preserved with the same solemn attention we reserve for the observable universe.
