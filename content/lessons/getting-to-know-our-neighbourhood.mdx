## Purpose

Children build vocabulary about the people and places they pass every day.
The walk supplies the real thing; the classroom follow-up asks each child to
represent one place or helper in their own way. The measure is growing
neighbourhood talk, not a finished product.

## Source-linked song use

- **All The Fish** — Mid North Coast Library (NSW, Australia) – *Baby Bounce Nursery Rhymes* (booklet). Use its repeated action verse as the walking warm-up on the carpet before you leave and as the settle-back song after.
- The linked topic material **Open Shut Them** makes a quiet fingers-and-listening opener while walking partners are assigned.

Keep to the printed booklet text; if you add local verses (crossing the road,
walking quietly), introduce them as your own class version, not source text.

## Prep and materials

- A short, pre-walked route with two or three fixed landmarks (a mailbox, a crossing guard, a garden, a shop window)
- One clipboard per adult with the noticing checklist below
- Paper and crayons at the carpet for the follow-up representation
- Your usual walk safety gear: vests, partner pairs, head-count routine

## Brief teaching sequence

1. **Warm up on the carpet (3 min).** Sing the walking warm-up (**All The Fish**), then name today's mission: "We are looking for helpers and places in our neighbourhood."
2. **Walk and notice (10 min).** Stop at each landmark. Ask open prompts: "Who works here? What do they do? Who has been inside?" Record children's exact words on the checklist — their phrasing becomes the lesson's word wall.
3. **Settle back (2 min).** Return using your stop/start routine; sing one final verse of the source song as the carpet signal.
4. **Represent one place (7 min).** Each child picks one thing they saw and shows it any way they choose: draw it, build it with two blocks, act out the helper's job, or tell a partner. Educators scribe one sentence per child verbatim.
5. **Share and close (3 min).** Three volunteers present. Read back two or three captured sentences from the walk, crediting the child who said them.

## Participation and access choices

- A child may represent their place by movement alone; the educator narrates what the movement shows so it enters the class record.
- Photos taken on the walk can be printed after and labelled instead of live drawing.
- Children who know the neighbourhood well can be route guides who point ahead; newcomers may simply look — both contribute vocabulary later.
- Invite families beforehand to name a place or helper that matters to them; greet those places by name on the walk.

## Teacher observation

Notice whether the child:

- names or points out a familiar place or helper unprompted;
- uses new neighbourhood vocabulary heard on the walk;
- connects a helper to what the helper does;
- represents their chosen place with words, image, or movement; and
- listens when a peer presents a different place.
